\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 sharply accelerated the diffusion of generative AI.  Within months, firms and workers were using these tools to perform tasks that had previously required paid human labor, including drafting documents, writing code, and summarizing information.  Adoption spread rapidly: survey evidence suggests that by late 2024, about one-quarter of workers had used generative AI tools in the workplace, and the pace of workplace adoption appeared to have been at least as fast as that of the personal computer.  Yet the diffusion of generative AI has not been uniform across occupations.  Early adoption rates were especially high in computer and mathematical, management, and business and finance jobs, while remaining much lower in many service, administrative, and blue-collar occupations \parencite{bick2024rapid}.  This uneven pattern suggests that the labor-market reactions to generative AI, if any, are also likely to be uneven.

Generative AI's effects on labor-market outcomes are not as straightforward as they seem. On the one hand, the new technology may raise productivity and expand output, increasing demand for tasks that remain complementary to human labor. On the other hand, it may substitute directly for workers in the tasks it can perform, putting downward pressure on the value of that work.  In a task-based view of production, generative AI acts as a shock to some tasks more than others, and the net effect on workers depends on how exposed their occupations are to that shift.

This paper studies the short-run labor-market effects of generative AI using worker-level data from the Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement (CPS-ASEC), linked to occupation-level AI exposure scores from \textcite{eloundou2024gpts}. To guide the analysis, I develop a simple toy model in which generative AI acts as a shock to a subset of tasks. I then exploit cross-occupation variation in exposure in an event-study difference-in-differences design covering 2018--2025, comparing outcomes in more- and less-exposed occupations before and after the release of ChatGPT.

I document two main findings. First, workers in more exposed occupations experience a relative increase in unemployment beginning in 2024. Second, beginning in 2025, log wages in more exposed occupations begin to follow a lower path relative to those in less exposed occupations, with the divergence concentrated among non-college workers. Further analysis suggests that this college/non-college wage asymmetry is driven primarily by occupational sorting rather than by differential wage responses between college and non-college workers within the same occupation.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:background}
%==================================================

Several recent empirical studies have begun to exploit cross-occupation or cross-firm variation in generative AI exposure to estimate the labor-market reactions to the new technology.  The literature has developed along two lines distinguished by the kinds of data they use.  One line of research relies on selective samples, where the treated group is narrow but relatively cleanly identified. Using LinkedIn resume data linked to job postings, \textcite{hosseini2025seniority} identify AI-adopting firms through their hiring of ``GenAI integrator'' roles and, in a difference-in-differences framework, find that junior employment at adopting firms falls by roughly 9 percent within a year, while senior employment remains unchanged; the effect appears to operate through slower hiring rather than increased layoffs. Using scraped employment histories from Upwork, a large online freelance platform, \textcite{hui2024short} find that workers in writing-related occupations experienced a 2 percent decline in monthly jobs and a 5 percent decline in monthly earnings after the release of ChatGPT. They also show that the release of text-to-image models such as DALL-E~2 and Midjourney generated comparable declines in design and illustration work. Importantly, these effects do not appear to be concentrated among lower-quality freelancers, suggesting that generative AI can substitute across a broad range of workers within exposed occupations.

Another line of research uses larger datasets that are more representative of the labor force, sacrificing some identification sharpness in exchange for broader coverage. \textcite{brynjolfsson2025canaries}, drawing on ADP payroll records through July 2025, find that employment among early-career workers, narrowly defined as ages 22--25, in highly AI-exposed occupations fell by roughly 13 percent after late 2022, while employment among older workers and those in less exposed occupations was stable or growing; wage effects are limited, suggesting that adjustment has operated more through employment rather than through wages. \textcite{chandar2025tracking}, using monthly CPS data from Q4 2022 through Q1 2025, finds little effect of exposure on aggregate employment or earnings growth. However, within the most AI-exposed quartile of occupations, occupations with higher college shares, such as software development, have continued to grow, while those with lower college shares, such as customer service, have contracted. 

This paper is closest to the second line of this literature, using nationally representative data to study broad labor-market adjustment across occupations with different levels of AI exposure.

\section{Toy Model}
\label{sec:model}
%==================================================
There is an established tradition in economics of analyzing how technological change affects labor market outcomes through the lens of \textit{tasks} in production. \textcite{autor2003skill} introduced this framework to document how computerization reallocates labor away from routine cognitive tasks, and \textcite{autor2013growth} extended it to explain the polarization of the US labor market due to routine-biased technological change. 
\textcite{autor2015whyjobs} further showed that automation need not reduce overall labor demand: because tasks are complementary within production, automating routine tasks increases the economic importance of the remaining tasks that require judgment or creativity, thereby sustaining demand for the workers who perform them.

Other researchers have applied a similar framework to immigration and offshoring: \textcite{ottaviano2013immigration}, for instance, use a task-based approach to study how relocating tasks abroad reallocates work across heterogeneous groups of local workers. The toy model developed here follows a similar logic. Rather than foreign labor performing a subset of tasks, generative AI does it. One might think of this as a form of ``offshoring to GPTopia.''

My model is deliberately stylized and highly simplified. It is meant to capture the short-run labor market adjustment following the workplace diffusion of generative AI, not the change in long-run general equilibrium. The goal of the model is to isolate two forces: expansion in aggregate output and dilution in task supply, and to provide a conceptual benchmark for interpreting the reduced-form evidence in the following sections of this paper.


\subsection{Setup}

Production requires a continuum of tasks indexed $i \in [0,1]$, ordered by susceptibility to AI. Tasks at the low end are cognitive tasks easily replicated by large language models (e.g., writing programs); tasks at the high end require human expertise that AI currently struggles to replicate (e.g., negotiating complex contracts).

Final output $Y$ aggregates tasks via a CES technology:
\begin{equation}
  Y = \left[\int_0^1 y(i)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\,di\right]^{\!\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}},
  \qquad \sigma > 0,
  \label{eq:ces}
\end{equation}
where $y(i)$ is the quantity of task $i$ used in production.

AI can additionally supply a subset of these tasks.  Write $s(i)\geq 0$
for the quantity of task~$i$ that AI contributes, with
\begin{equation}
  s'(i) < 0 \;\text{ for } i<I^*,
  \qquad
  s(i) = 0 \;\text{ for } i \geq I^*,
  \label{eq:ai_supply}
\end{equation}
where $I^*\in(0,1)$ is the \textit{AI capability frontier}.  Tasks below $I^*$
can theoretically be supplied by AI, with the contribution strictly decreasing in
task complexity; tasks at or above $I^*$ remain beyond AI's reach.  As AI
improves, $I^*$ shifts rightward and $s$ may increase throughout its support.

\begin{assumption}[Fixed Output Price]
  The output price $P_Y$ is exogenous and fixed.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Full Specialization]
  Workers are indexed by ability type $i^*\in[0,1]$.  A worker of type $i^*$ fully specializes in task
  $i^*$---the task at which they hold comparative
  advantage over other types.  Each task $i$ is therefore supplied exclusively by workers of
  type $i^*=i$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Inelastic Labor Supply]
  The density of worker types $f(i)$ over $[0,1]$ is fixed and exogenous.  Workers do not
  adjust their type in response to wages, so the pre-AI supply of task $i$ is just $f(i)$
  regardless of the task price.
\end{assumption}

\noindent Competitive firms take $P_Y$ and task prices $\{p_0(i)\}$ as given and minimize the
cost of producing $Y$ subject to~\eqref{eq:ces}.  The equilibrium task prices and wages are derived
in Appendix~\ref{app:foc}.





\subsection{Pre-AI Wages}

Before AI, cost minimization subject to \eqref{eq:ces} yields equilibrium
task prices (derived in Appendix~\ref{app:foc}):
\begin{equation}
  p_0(i) = P_Y \cdot \left(\frac{Y_0}{f(i)}\right)^{\!1/\sigma},
  \label{eq:taskprice_preai}
\end{equation}
where $Y_0\equiv\bigl[\int_0^1 f(i)^{(\sigma-1)/\sigma}di\bigr]^{\sigma/(\sigma-1)}$
is pre-AI aggregate output, fully determined by the distribution of worker
types $f$ via the CES aggregator. In this equilibrium, a
worker of type $i^*$ earns:
\begin{equation}
  w_0(i^*) = p_0(i^*).
  \label{eq:wage_preai}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:preai} illustrates this wage schedule. The figure
plots $p_0(i)$ as linearly increasing in $i$ --- this is only for visual clarity, the model
imposes no explicit restriction on the shape of $p_0$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{output/model/fig_preai.png}
  \caption{Pre-AI equilibrium wage schedule. Each worker of type $i^*$ earns the competitive task price $w_0(i^*) = p_0(i^*)$. The downward slope in $f$ is assumed for illustration only; the model places no explicit restriction on the distribution of initial task supply.}
  \label{fig:preai}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Effects of the AI Supply Shock}

When AI enters the economy, it augments the supply side of each task
market it can serve.  The effective supply of task~$i$ becomes
\begin{equation}
  \bar y(i) \;=\; f(i) + s(i),
  \label{eq:effective_supply}
\end{equation}
where $s(i)>0$ for $i<I^*$ (AI contributes to supply) and $s(i)=0$
for $i\geq I^*$ (beyond AI's capability).  Because some task supplies
have risen while others have not, the CES aggregator yields a new
level of aggregate output
\begin{equation}
  \bar Y \;=\; \left[\int_0^1 \bar y(i)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}}\,di
               \right]^{\!\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}}
  \;>\; Y_0,
  \label{eq:Ybar}
\end{equation}
and by the same cost-minimization logic as before
(Appendix~\ref{app:foc}), the post-AI task price is
\begin{equation}
  p_1(i) \;=\; P_Y\!\left(\frac{\bar Y}{\bar y(i)}\right)^{\!1/\sigma}.
  \label{eq:taskprice_postai}
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Post-AI wages.}
Since $w_1(i^*)=p_1(i^*)$, the post-AI wage is derived directly from
\eqref{eq:taskprice_postai}:
\begin{equation}
  w_1(i^*) \;=\; P_Y\!\left(\frac{\bar Y}{f(i^*)+s(i^*)}\right)^{\!1/\sigma}.
  \label{eq:wage_postai}
\end{equation}
Taking the ratio to the pre-AI benchmark
$w_0(i^*)=P_Y\bigl(Y_0/f(i^*)\bigr)^{1/\sigma}$ yields
\begin{equation}
  \frac{w_1(i^*)}{w_0(i^*)}
  \;=\;
  \underbrace{\left(\frac{\bar Y}{Y_0}\right)^{\!1/\sigma}}_{\text{output expansion}}
  \cdot
  \underbrace{\left(\frac{f(i^*)}{f(i^*)+s(i^*)}\right)^{\!1/\sigma}}_{\text{supply dilution}}.
  \label{eq:wage_ratio}
\end{equation}
Two competing forces govern the wage change.  The first factor exceeds one: AI adds task inputs to the production, aggregate output $\bar Y$ rises above $Y_0$.  The second factor is at most one: tasks that AI can supply ($i^*<I^*$) see their effective supply $f+s$ rise above $f$, diluting the task supply.  For workers specializing in tasks above the capability frontier ($i^*\geq I^*$), $s(i^*)=0$ and only the output-expansion factor applies; for workers specializing in tasks below $I^*$, the net effect on their wage is ambiguous: it depends on the local $s(i^*)/f(i^*)$ ratio as well as the aggregate output gain $\bar Y / Y_0$, which is itself determined by the full profile of task supply $f(\cdot), s(\cdot)$, and by the elasticity of substitution between tasks in production ($\sigma$).


However, the relative effect is unambiguous. Workers above the capability frontier ($i^* \geq I^*$) receive only the output-expansion gain, while workers below ($i^* < I^*$) receive the same gain net of supply dilution. The gap between exposed and unexposed workers therefore widens, regardless of whether exposed wages fall in absolute terms. This is the prediction that the empirical analysis will test.

Figure~\ref{fig:postai} provides a graphical illustration of these effects.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{output/model/fig_postai.png}
  \caption{Post-AI shock under two AI supply intensities.  Left panel: task supply $f(i)$ (blue) and $f(i)+s(i)$ (dashed).  Right panel: pre-AI wage $w_0$ (dashed blue) vs.\ post-AI wage $w_1$ (solid).  Workers deep inside $[0,I^*)$ with large $s/f$ see wage compression; workers near $I^*$ with small $s/f$ can still gain from output expansion; workers above $I^*$ gain uniformly.}

\begin{remark}[Task bundling]
The derivation above treats each worker as a single-task specialist of type $i^*$. We may instead let a worker perform multiple tasks, organized as an occupation-level bundle with fixed time shares $\theta_k^x$ on task $k$, where $\sum_k \theta_k^x = 1$. A worker in occupation $x$ earns the wage
\begin{equation}
  w_x \;=\; \sum_k \theta_k^x \, p_k,
  \label{eq:bundle}
\end{equation}
  The wage-ratio result of the preceding analysis carries through, so long as the task weights ($\theta_k^x$) are exogenous.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Displacement]
The full-specialization and inelastic-labor-supply assumptions rule out workers switching task types in response to wages, which keeps the model intact but does not fully describe the labor market response to the technology shock.  In reality, wages are sticky downward: firms often cannot cut pay to the new market-clearing level, so part of the adjustment shifts from the intensive margin (wages) to the extensive margin (employment).  Firms faced with compressed task prices slow hiring into exposed roles and lay off workers, and workers whose reservation wage exceeds the post-AI offer may exit their current jobs voluntarily.  A more formal model would require endogenous worker mobility, wage-setting frictions, and a reservation-wage mechanism, none of which the toy model incorporates.
\end{remark}

With the toy model's prediction in hand, that workers whose tasks are heavily exposed to AI fare worse in relative terms than workers in unexposed occupations, and that displacement is the labor market's likely outlet when wages cannot fall far enough, I turn to empirical analysis in the following sections.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{CPS-ASEC}

The primary data source is the Annual Social and Economic Supplement to the Current Population Survey (CPS-ASEC), accessed through IPUMS \parencite{ipumscps2025}. The CPS-ASEC is a nationally representative annual survey that captures labor-force status, occupation, wage income, and detailed demographic characteristics for approximately 100,000 households. I use eight survey waves covering income reference years 2018--2025, spanning both the pre- and post-ChatGPT periods.

I restrict the sample to respondents aged 22--65 who are active in the labor force --- either employed or actively seeking work. Workers with less than a high school diploma are excluded. After merging with the AI exposure variable and dropping observations with missing values, the main estimation sample contains 500,669 person-year observations.

\paragraph{Unemployment.} The unemployment indicator equals one if a respondent is classified as unemployed by the CPS --- not working but actively searching for work (CPS \texttt{empstat} codes 21 and 22) --- and zero otherwise. The sample mean is 3.4\%, roughly consistent with official U.S. unemployment statistics over this period.

\paragraph{Wages.} For the wage analysis, I use annual real wage and salary income (\texttt{incwage}), deflated to 1982 dollars using the annual average CPI-U from FRED \parencite{fred_cpiaucsl}. The outcome variable is the natural log of real wages. Observations with zero or missing wage income are excluded from the wage sample (workers who are unemployed or out of the labor force for wage purposes), yielding 464,300 observations.

\paragraph{Potential experience.} For the experience heterogeneity specification, I impute potential labor-market experience as $\max(\text{age} - \text{years of schooling} - 6,\, 0)$, with years of schooling approximated from the CPS education levels.

\subsection{AI Exposure}

The AI exposure variable is drawn from \textcite{eloundou2024gpts}, which constructs occupation-level LLM exposure scores from the O*NET 27.2 database. O*NET provides two complementary task-level resources: 19,265 \textit{occupational tasks} (occupation-specific units of work tied to a particular occupation) and 2,087 \textit{Detailed Work Activities} (DWAs) --- broader action categories that may be associated with one or more tasks across occupations.

\paragraph{Exposure rubric.}
Annotators assess each task or DWA on a three-category rubric, asking whether access to an LLM could reduce the time required to complete it by at least 50\% at equivalent quality:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{E0} (no exposure): the LLM yields no meaningful time reduction, or degrades output quality.
  \item \textbf{E1} (direct exposure): a standard LLM interface, like ChatGPT or the OpenAI Playground, reduces task time by at least 50\% on its own.
  \item \textbf{E2} (LLM-augmented exposure): the LLM alone cannot meet the threshold of 50\% time reduction in the task, but software built on top of the LLM could do so.
\end{itemize}
Where tasks are linked to DWAs, annotators rated the DWA rather than the task directly, and each DWA's rating was applied to every task associated with it.  Task-level ratings are then aggregated up to the occupation level.

\paragraph{Occupation-level aggregation.}
For each occupation, O*NET designates each task as \textit{core} (weight $w_t = 2$) or \textit{supplemental} (weight $w_t = 1$). Three summary measures vary how E1 and E2 tasks are scored:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Occupation Score} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t \in T_o} w_t \cdot s_t}{\displaystyle\sum_{t \in T_o} w_t},
  \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
where $s_t = 1$ for E1 tasks, $s_t = 0.5$ for E2 tasks, and $s_t = 0$ for E0 tasks. The \textit{alpha} score ($\alpha_o$) sets $s_t = 0$ for all E2 tasks, counting only direct LLM exposure. The \textit{zeta} score ($\zeta_o$) sets $s_t = 1$ for E2 tasks, providing an upper bound. $\beta_o$ is the weighted middle ground: E2 tasks are discounted by half, reflecting the additional efforts required to build and deploy LLM-powered software on top of the base model.

\paragraph{Annotators.}
\textcite{eloundou2024gpts} collect two independent sets of annotations. \textit{Human ratings} --- ratings produced by the authors and experienced alignment annotators --- are the primary benchmark; they were applied to DWAs and aggregated to the task level. \textit{GPT-4 ratings} were obtained by prompting an early GPT-4 version directly on all task/occupation pairs, serving as a validation dataset. The two sources are highly correlated at the occupation level.

I use \texttt{dv\_rating\_beta}: the \textit{GPT-4-annotated} $\beta$ score. Applying a single model uniformly across all task/occupation pairs better preserves internal consistency. The cost is potential overconfidence in GPT-4's assessment of its own capabilities. $\beta$ is preferred to $\alpha$ (which ignores the ecosystem of LLM-powered tools already deployed or in development at the time of study) and $\zeta$ (which assumes a hypothetically well-developed AI infrastructure). $\beta$ is therefore the most economically meaningful measure for the post-ChatGPT period.  

Although $\beta_o$ is constructed from annotator judgments rather than observed workplace use, \textcite{bick2024rapid} provide some external validation: using the Real-Time Population Survey, a nationally representative survey of U.S. workers, they report a correlation of $\rho = 0.67$ between \textcite{eloundou2024gpts}'s ChatGPT-$\beta$ exposure score and self-reported generative AI adoption at the occupation-group level, supporting the interpretation of $\beta_o$ as a proxy for actual workplace adoption of generative AI tools.


Because CPS-ASEC codes occupations using a Census-based scheme which is distinct from the SOC occupation codes used by \textcite{eloundou2024gpts}, I construct a hierarchical crosswalk: from Census 2010 occupation codes to 6-digit 2010 SOC codes, then from 2010 SOC to 2018 SOC using the BLS official crosswalk \parencite{bls_soc_crosswalk_2018}. Where exact 6-digit matches are unavailable, exposure scores are aggregated at the 5-digit and 4-digit SOC levels, taking group means. This procedure retains nearly all observations in the sample.

The resulting $\beta_o$ is time-invariant and assigned at the occupation level.  In the CPS-ASEC sample, $\beta_o$ has a mean of $0.37$ and a standard deviation of $0.21$, ranging from $0$ to $0.975$; the full distribution and its education-specific statistics are reported in Table~\ref{tab:beta_summary}.

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}

\subsection{Main Specification}

The core empirical question is whether outcomes for workers in high-exposure occupations moved differently from outcomes in low-exposure occupations after ChatGPT's release. The estimating equation is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:main}
Y_{it} = \alpha
  + \sum_{t \neq 2022} \beta_t \left( \mathbf{1}[t] \times \text{AIExposure}_i \right)
  + \text{lower-order terms} + X_{it}'\Gamma + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $Y_{it}$ is either the unemployment indicator or log wages for worker $i$ in year $t$; $\text{AIExposure}_i$ is worker $i$'s occupation's AI exposure score; and year 2022 is the omitted category. The lower-order terms include year fixed effects and the main effect of AI exposure. The $\beta_t$ coefficients trace, year by year, whether high-exposure workers diverge from low-exposure workers relative to the 2022 baseline. If AI caused labor-market disruption after its diffusion, the post-2022 $\beta_t$ should be positive (for unemployment) or negative (for wages), while the pre-2022 $\beta_t$ should hover near zero.

Five progressively stricter specifications are estimated: (1) no additional controls, (2) sex fixed effects, (3) sex and education fixed effects, (4) sex and education fixed effects plus state-by-year fixed effects, and (5) sex and education fixed effects plus state-by-year and industry-by-year fixed effects. Specification (5) is the preferred specification: it absorbs both state-specific and industry-specific time shocks, so that the estimated post-ChatGPT divergence in outcomes is identified from variation along the occupational exposure dimension net of geographic and sectoral time trajectories. Standard errors are clustered at the occupation level in all specifications to allow for heteroskedasticity and within-occupation correlation in the error term, particularly because $\beta_o$ is assigned at the occupation level.

The coefficient $\hat\beta_t$ can be read as a reduced-form summary of how the relationship between occupational AI exposure and labor-market outcomes in year $t$ differs from that relationship in the 2022 baseline year. As \textcite{callaway2024continuous} show, coefficients from continuous-treatment two-way fixed effects specifications need not correspond to simple population-average per-unit treatment effects. Under treatment-effect heterogeneity, they can instead aggregate underlying exposure-specific effects using regression-implied weights that may not reflect the population distribution of exposure. Accordingly, I interpret the estimates as reduced-form summaries of relative outcome movements across the exposure distribution, rather than as strictly causal per-unit effects of AI exposure.

This event-study specification is preferred to a simple Treatment $\times$ Post specification for two reasons. First, generative AI capabilities improved substantially across the post-ChatGPT window, as successive frontier models were released (Figure~\ref{fig:model_timeline}). Second, workplace adoption of generative AI tools rose gradually rather than all at once: \textcite{bick2024rapid} document that the share of U.S.\ workers using generative AI climbed from near zero at the end of 2022 to roughly one-quarter by late 2024. Taken together, these features imply that the realized intensity of the AI shock on the supply of tasks likely varied over time. The same occupation-level exposure score may correspond to a relatively weak treatment in 2023, when tools were less capable and adoption was still limited, but a stronger treatment by 2024 or 2025 as capabilities and take-up increased. A Treatment $\times$ Post specification would collapse this evolving treatment intensity into a single average post-period effect, whereas the year-by-year coefficients ${\hat\beta_t}$ allow the adjustment process to be traced directly.

\subsection{Heterogeneity: Experience and Education}

\paragraph{Experience.} The experience heterogeneity analysis is inspired by \textcite{hosseini2025seniority} and \textcite{brynjolfsson2025canaries}, both of which find that generative AI acts as a seniority-biased technological change. Each paper, however, looks at a limited slice of the labor market: \textcite{hosseini2025seniority} draw on a selective sample of firms that publicly hired for ``GenAI integrator'' roles, and \textcite{brynjolfsson2025canaries} identify the effect on a narrowly defined 22--25 age band. I test whether the same seniority gradient appears in a nationally representative CPS-ASEC sample using a continuous measure of labor-market experience. The specification supplements the main equation with a triple interaction:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:triple}
Y_{it} = \alpha
  + \sum_{t \neq 2022} \delta_t \left( \mathbf{1}[t] \times \text{Experience}_i \times \text{AIExposure}_i \right)
  + \text{lower-order terms} + X_{it}'\Gamma + \varepsilon_{it}.
\end{equation}
The sign of post-2022 $\delta_t$ is interpreted with respect to the outcome: more-experienced workers are relatively shielded if $\delta_t > 0$ for log wages and if $\delta_t < 0$ for unemployment.


\paragraph{Education.} The education heterogeneity analysis is motivated by the descriptive patterns documented in Section~\ref{sec:data}. Because $\beta_o$ is assigned at the occupation level, and because college share varies within narrow ranges of $\beta_o$, college and non-college workers with similar measured exposure may still sort into different occupations. The college / non-college split is therefore not a proxy for the exposure split, and differences in outcomes between the two groups need not simply reflect differences in exposure per se. To examine this dimension, I divide the sample into \textit{college-educated} workers (BA or above, educ $>$ 110) and \textit{non-college} workers (high school or some college, educ $\leq$ 110) and estimate the main specification \eqref{eq:main} separately within each group:



\begin{equation}
\label{eq:split}
Y_{it}^{\,g} = \alpha^g
  + \sum_{t \neq 2022} \beta_t^g \left( \mathbf{1}[t] \times \text{AIExposure}_i \right)
  + \text{lower-order terms} + X_{it}'\Gamma^g + \varepsilon_{it}^g, \quad g \in \{\text{college},\;\text{non-college}\}.
\end{equation}
Each subsample has its own baseline, trend, and exposure gradient, and the two coefficient paths $\{\hat\beta_t^{\text{col}}\}$ and $\{\hat\beta_t^{\text{noncol}}\}$ can be compared directly.

\section{Results and Interpretation}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Unemployment}

Figure~\ref{fig:unemployment_stateYFE_plot} plots the event-study coefficients for unemployment in the preferred specification, which absorbs sex, education, state$\times$year, and industry$\times$year fixed effects.  The full regression results across all five specifications are reported in Table~\ref{tab:unemployment_main}.

Pre-ChatGPT coefficients for 2018 and 2019 are close to zero and statistically insignificant.  The 2020 and 2021 coefficients, by contrast, are significantly negative.  This reflects the differential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic: high-exposure occupations, typically cognitively heavy positions, were largely remote- and hybrid-compatible and hence were partly shielded from the layoffs that concentrated in in-person service and manually heavy work. The 2020--2021 pattern is therefore a pre-existing feature of the comparison between high- and low-exposure occupations, rather than evidence for a parallel-trends violation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{unemployment_indFE_plot.pdf}
\caption{Unemployment: DiD coefficients (year $\times$ AI exposure), preferred specification (sex, education, state$\times$year, and industry$\times$year fixed effects). Year 2022 is the baseline ($\hat\beta_{2022} = 0$). Error bars are 95\% CI, SEs clustered at the occupation level.}
\label{fig:unemployment_stateYFE_plot}
\end{center}

Post-2022, a positive unemployment effect of AI exposure emerges. In the preferred specification, the 2023 coefficient is essentially zero; the 2024 coefficient rises to $+0.0113$ ($p<0.05$) and the 2025 coefficient is $+0.0140$ ($p<0.05$).  The pattern is stable across all five specifications.

The unemployment response is also consistent with the remark about displacement outlined in Section~\ref{sec:model}. In the model, AI’s supply-dilution force compresses the value of exposed tasks, but downward wage stickiness limits adjustment through wages alone. Part of the adjustment therefore shifts to the extensive margin, displacing workers from exposed positions. The statistically significant post-2022 unemployment response is consistent with this mechanism.

%==================================================
\subsection{Wages}
%==================================================

Figure~\ref{fig:logwage_stateYFE_plot} plots the event-study coefficients for log real wages in the preferred specification; the full regression results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:logwage_main}. The pre-ChatGPT coefficients for 2018, 2019, and 2020 are uniformly small and statistically insignificant across all five specifications, broadly consistent with parallel wage trends. The 2021 coefficient is positive ($\approx +0.05$) and marginally significant at the 10 percent level in specifications that include education and state-by-year fixed effects, but it loses statistical significance in the strictest specification once industry-by-year fixed effects are added. I interpret this pattern as suggestive of the same COVID-recovery asymmetry flagged in the unemployment results: wages in high-exposure occupations appear to have rebounded faster than those in less-exposed occupations as the pandemic receded, but this pre-period difference is not robust to absorbing industry-specific time shocks. 


\begin{figure}[htbp]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{logwage_indFE_plot.pdf}
\caption{Log real wage: DiD coefficients (year $\times$ AI exposure), preferred specification (sex, education, state$\times$year, and industry$\times$year fixed effects). Year 2022 is the baseline ($\hat\beta_{2022} = 0$). Error bars are 95\% CI, SEs clustered at the occupation level.}
\label{fig:logwage_stateYFE_plot}
\end{center}
\end{figure}
Post-ChatGPT, the wage adjustment is weaker than the unemployment adjustment and emerges only late in the sample. In the naive and sex-FE specifications, the 2025 coefficient is $-0.035$ and $-0.045$ respectively, neither significant at conventional levels.  The two most demanding specifications yield statistically significant 2025 coefficients: the spec absorbing sex, education, and state$\times$year fixed effects gives $-0.059$ ($p<0.05$), and the preferred spec further absorbing industry$\times$year fixed effects gives $-0.061$ ($p<0.05$).  No significant effect appears in 2023 or 2024 under any specification.

The delayed wage response is consistent with the displacement channel discussed in the unemployment results: with wages downward-sticky, adjustment flows first through the extensive margin (employment), and the intensive margin (wages) adjustment lags behind.

The fact that statistical significance requires state$\times$year fixed effects is itself interesting. On the one hand, this could suggest a potential weakness of the wage result: the estimate is not robust across all five specifications. On the other hand, this could suggest that noise from region-specific shocks in 2025 may have been large enough to mask the exposure signal in looser specifications. I note both interpretations as possible and do not strongly favor one over the other.

My second concern is that 2025 is the end of the sample. Whether the relative wage difference persists in 2026 is an empirical question the current data cannot answer. Readers should interpret the 2025 result with that uncertainty in mind.

Finally, the wage result should be interpreted in relative terms, in line with the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:model}. A negative estimate does not imply that real wages fell in absolute terms for high-$\beta_o$ workers, or indeed that wages fell at all. Aggregate wages may have risen for all occupations post-2022, and the coefficient would still be negative if they rose by less in high-exposure occupations than in low-exposure ones. What the estimate captures is the differential wage path along the $\beta_o$ distribution post-2022. 
%==================================================
\subsection{Experience Heterogeneity}
%==================================================

Tables~\ref{tab:unemp_exphet} and~\ref{tab:lw_exphet} report the experience heterogeneity results. The triple-interaction coefficients on year $\times$ experience $\times$ AI exposure are small and statistically insignificant for 2024 and 2025 across all five specifications, for both unemployment and log wages. One 2023 log-wage coefficient reaches marginal significance in some specifications, but this pattern is not stable across later years: the 2024 and 2025 coefficients are small and insignificant throughout. Overall, the experience heterogeneity results are largely null; I find little evidence that labor-market experience meaningfully changes workers’ sensitivity to occupational AI exposure.

The null results provide an interesting contrast to \textcite{hosseini2025seniority} and \textcite{brynjolfsson2025canaries}. Two considerations may help reconcile the difference. First, \textcite{hosseini2025seniority} study AI-adopting firms identified through public ``GenAI integrator'' postings, which may over-represent tech-forward adopters and therefore capture a more selected margin of adjustment than the nationally representative CPS-ASEC sample. Second, \textcite{brynjolfsson2025canaries} find that their seniority gradient is sharply concentrated among workers aged 22--25, with insignificant estimates for older cohorts. A continuous-experience interaction in CPS-ASEC naturally averages over the full experience distribution, so a localized entry-level effect could be diluted. Thus, the CPS-ASEC evidence does not directly contradict these findings; rather, it suggests that experience is not a broad margin of heterogeneity in the population-wide early labor-market response to generative AI.

Another reason my estimation using CPS-ASEC may understate the entry-level unemployment effect is mechanical: $\beta_o$ is assigned at the occupational level, so workers enter the unemployment regression only if they carry an occupation code (which in CPS-ASEC requires a previous job).  Workers who have never held a first job, including recent graduates still searching, are in the labor force by definition but fall outside the sample used here. 


%==================================================
\subsection{Education Heterogeneity}
%==================================================

While the experience heterogeneity analysis yields largely null results, splitting the sample by education groups reveals an asymmetry that is less visible in the pooled specification. Both the unemployment and wage responses to AI exposure are concentrated among non-college workers, whereas the corresponding coefficients for college-educated workers are statistically indistinguishable from zero on both margins.

On the unemployment margin, in the preferred specification, the 2024 coefficient is $-0.002$ for college-educated workers (BA or above) and $+0.017$ ($p<0.05$) for non-college workers (high school or some college); the corresponding 2025 coefficients are $+0.004$ and $+0.016$ ($p<0.05$) (Tables~\ref{tab:unemp_col} and~\ref{tab:unemp_noncol}). Thus, the unemployment response is concentrated among non-college workers. Figure~\ref{fig:unemp_stateYFE_colComb} plots the two coefficient paths side by side.

The wage response shows the same pattern. The 2025 log-wage coefficient is $-0.070$ ($p<0.05$) for non-college workers in the preferred specification, while the corresponding college-worker coefficients are indistinguishable from zero in every post-2022 year ($+0.050$ in 2023, $+0.073$ in 2024, and $-0.024$ in 2025; Tables~\ref{tab:lw_col} and~\ref{tab:lw_noncol}).  Figure~\ref{fig:lw_stateYFE_colComb} plots the two coefficient paths side by side; the non-college 2025 compression stands in sharp contrast to the flat college path.

Year 2022 is the baseline.  The non-college 2025 compression ($-0.070^{**}$) stands in contrast to the flat college path.  Error bars are 95\% CI, SEs clustered at the occupation level.}
\label{fig:lw_stateYFE_colComb}
\end{center}
\end{figure}
The asymmetry on both margins invites two interpretations that the split-sample approach in \eqref{eq:split} cannot distinguish. The first is a between-occupation story. Because $\beta_o$ is assigned at the occupation level, college and non-college workers with similar measured exposure may still be sorted into systematically different occupations. Section~\ref{sec:data} already showed that the college share of an occupation varies widely even within narrow bands of $\beta_o$. On this reading, the education gap in both responses reflects sorting across occupations: occupational attributes correlated with college share, but not captured by the exposure score, generate different post-2022 outcome paths. What appears to be heterogeneous sensitivity to AI exposure by education may therefore reflect differences across occupations that $\beta_o$ alone fails to capture. The second interpretation is a within-occupation story. Holding occupation fixed, college-educated workers may be more productive, more adaptable, or better positioned to use generative AI, leaving them better shielded from both wage compression and unemployment pressure. In that case, the education gap would reflect individual-level heterogeneity that survives conditioning on occupation.


The two readings are observationally equivalent in the split-sample regressions, but they are separable by the following specification, estimated separately for log wages and unemployment:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:within_occ}
Y_{it} = \sum_{t\ne 2022} \gamma_t\,(\mathbf{1}\{\text{year}=t\}\times \text{AIExposure}_{o(i)} \times \mathrm{Col}_i) + \text{lower-order terms} + \alpha_{o(i),t} + X_{it}'\Gamma + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $Y_{it}$ is either log real wages or the unemployment indicator, $\mathrm{Col}_i$ is an indicator for worker $i$ being college-educated, $\alpha_{o,t}$ is a full occupation$\times$year fixed effect, $X_{it}$ absorbs sex, education, state$\times$year, and industry$\times$year fixed effects, and standard errors are clustered at the occupation level.  The occupation$\times$year fixed effect absorbs any variation in $Y_{it}$ common to all workers in occupation $o$ in year $t$, including the occupation-level exposure path itself.  The coefficient of interest, $\gamma_t$, therefore isolates the differential response of college-educated workers within the same occupation-year cell.  If the within-occupation reading is right, $\gamma_t$ should be positive for wages and negative for unemployment in the post-2022 years; if the between-occupation reading is right, the selection that drives the split-sample asymmetry is absorbed into $\alpha_{o,t}$ and $\gamma_t$ should be indistinguishable from zero on both margins.

Figures~\ref{fig:lw_within_plot} and~\ref{fig:unemp_within_plot} plot the estimated $\gamma_t$ for 2018--2025 for log wages and unemployment respectively, with both regressions reported in Table~\ref{tab:lw_within_occ}.  In both outcomes, the post-2022 coefficients are small and statistically indistinguishable from zero: for log wages, $\gamma_t$ is $-0.052$ in 2023, $+0.002$ in 2024, and $+0.002$ in 2025 (standard errors $0.061$, $0.059$, $0.072$); for unemployment, $\gamma_t$ is $+0.004$, $-0.006$, and $+0.002$ respectively (standard errors $0.017$, $0.015$, $0.012$).  Within the same occupation-year cell, then, college-educated and non-college workers do not experience systematically different wage or unemployment responses to AI exposure, and the education gap visible in the split-sample regressions does not survive occupation$\times$year fixed effects.  The evidence therefore points to the between-occupation reading: the education gap in both the wage and unemployment responses reflects occupation sorting on dimensions correlated with college share but not captured by $\beta_o$, rather than an individual-level productivity gap within occupations.

Why do between-occupation differences generate different post-2022 wage and unemployment paths? One possible explanation is intra-bundle effort reallocation. Remark~1 in Section~\ref{sec:model} held the task bundle weights $\theta^x$ fixed for each worker by assumption. 
However, if workers can instead adjust $\theta^x$ in response to the shock by reallocating time and effort away from AI-exposed tasks and toward less exposed tasks, then the wage effect on the effective task bundle can be attenuated.  

College-educated workers in high-exposure occupations may be better positioned along this margin. 
This could reflect their (i) greater heterogeneity in task exposure within the occupation, creating larger scope for effort reallocation across tasks, or (ii) greater flexibility in adjusting effort across tasks within the bundle, or both.
Both channels here are offered as conjectures. That said, existing literature points in a similar direction.

\textcite{hampole2025ai} formalize within-occupation task reallocation in response to technology shocks, showing that workers’ capacity to offset task displacement depends on how exposure is distributed across tasks. \textcite{autor2025expertise} offer a related but different angle: AI’s wage effect depends not only on how many tasks in an occupation are exposed, but on which kinds of tasks are exposed. In other words, AI may automate either the routine, easy-to-delegate parts of a job or the skill-defining parts that previously made the job require specialized expertise. In the first case, the remaining human work may become more ``expertise-intensive'', potentially protecting wages and employment for workers who are already holding those jobs. In the second case, AI may lower the expertise threshold required to perform the job, expanding the pool of workers able to compete for those positions and placing downward pressure on wages. One interpretation of the education split is that college-heavy, high-exposure occupations are more likely to resemble the first case, while non-college-heavy, high-exposure occupations are more vulnerable to the second. I treat this mechanism as suggestive rather than definitive, since the  exposure measure in \textcite{eloundou2024gpts} does not distinguish whether the exposed tasks are routine/inexpert tasks or skill-defining/expert tasks.

A secondary possibility is that part of the pattern reflects shifts in output demand not fully captured in the regression. After 2022, demand may have increased for goods and services produced disproportionately in high-exposure, college-intensive occupations, partly offsetting wage pressure for college-educated workers. The preferred specification partially addresses broad demand-side explanations by including industry-by-year fixed effects, which absorb industry-specific time shocks. However, these fixed effects do not rule out more granular demand shifts within industries, such as rising demand for AI-complementary occupations or firms that disproportionately employ college-educated workers.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper studies the short-run labor-market effects of generative AI in the years following the public release of ChatGPT. The results support a view of generative AI’s early labor-market effects as an uneven revaluation of tasks rather than a simple productivity shock. In the toy model, AI raises output by augmenting the supply of some tasks while simultaneously diluting the relative value of the other tasks that it can assist or substitute. 

The empirical results are consistent with this logic. Two main findings emerge from the analysis. First, workers in more exposed occupations experience a relative increase in unemployment beginning in 2024, with this extensive-margin response concentrated among non-college workers. This response appears earlier than the wage response, consistent with a setting in which downward wage stickiness limits immediate adjustment through pay and shifts part of the response to displacement. Second, by 2025, wages in more exposed occupations also begin to diverge onto a lower path, with this intensive-margin adjustment again concentrated among non-college workers, whereas college-educated workers in exposed occupations show no comparable change in either margin. Further analysis indicates that the education gap on both margins is driven primarily by occupational sorting on dimensions the exposure score does not capture, rather than by differential responses within the same occupation.


Taken together, the evidence suggests that the early labor-market effects of generative AI are not best understood as an immediate, uniform wage or employment shock. Instead, they appear to be unfolding as an uneven process of adjustment across occupations and worker groups. As the technology continues to diffuse, understanding these occupational and distributional margins of generative AI's impact will be essential for interpreting both its broader social gains, in the form of potential output expansion, and its labor-market costs, including worker displacement and the frictions associated with adjustment toward a new long run equilibrium.

\section{Derivation of Wage Schedules}
\label{app:foc}

\noindent\textbf{Step 1.} Cost minimization $\to$ FOC:
$\;p_0(i)=\mu\cdot(Y/y(i))^{1/\sigma}$, \quad $\mu=P_Y$ in competitive equilibrium.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 2.} Task demand:
$\;y^d(i)=Y(P_Y/p_0(i))^{\sigma}$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 3.} Market clearing $y^d(i)=f(i)$:
\[
  p_0(i) = P_Y\!\left(\frac{Y}{f(i)}\right)^{\!1/\sigma}.
\]

\noindent\textbf{Step 4.} Plug $y(i)=f(i)$ into CES $\to$ output is pinned:
\[
  Y_0 = \left[\int_0^1 f(i)^{(\sigma-1)/\sigma}\,di
  \right]^{\!\sigma/(\sigma-1)}.
\]
$Y_0$ depends only on worker distribution $f$ --- not on prices. $\blacksquare$

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 5.} Post-AI: effective supply
$\bar y(i)=f(i)+s(i)$.  Repeat Steps 1--4 with $\bar y$ in place of $f$:
\[
  \bar Y = \left[\int_0^1 (f(i)+s(i))^{(\sigma-1)/\sigma}\,di
  \right]^{\!\sigma/(\sigma-1)} > Y_0,
  \qquad
  p_1(i) = P_Y\!\left(\frac{\bar Y}{f(i)+s(i)}\right)^{\!1/\sigma}.
  \quad\blacksquare
\]

\paragraph{Summary.}
The unemployment findings are fully robust to binarization.  The full-sample wage finding is robust.  The split-sample wage finding (2025 non-college compression) is directionally robust across all specifications but loses statistical significance under the strictest binarized specification.
